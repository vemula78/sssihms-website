Today, I will talk about diabetes in Indians and share some lessons we have learned over the last few years.

Last year, I delivered the Kelly West Lecture at the American Diabetes Association. They asked me to speak on this topic at the ADA, and my lecture was published in the February issue of Diabetes Care. The U.S. was very interested in the fact that South Asian diabetes seems to be different, and wanted to know what lessons we have learned that might apply not only to India and South Asia, but to many parts of the world.

If you look at the prevalence of diabetes in India, in 1972, when I was 18 and joined my father in medical school, a survey by the Indian Council of Medical Research found that the prevalence of diabetes was 2% in urban areas and 1% in rural areas, among people aged 20 and above. Gradually, the prevalence increased.

The green dotted line on the graph marks 1991, a significant year when Dr. Manmohan Singh, then Finance Minister, opened up India’s economy. The socioeconomic status of people rose, which had many benefits, but also brought a sharp increase in diabetes and obesity rates.

The next study showed prevalence in double figures—11.6%. Since then, the prevalence has continued to rise. The last citywide study we did in Delhi and Chennai was in 2015, when it had already reached 25%. That means one in four people aged 20 or above in these cities has diabetes. If you look at those aged 50 and above, the prevalence is 50%. In other words, in Chennai or Delhi, half of the people over 50 have diabetes, regardless of socioeconomic status, and another 25% have prediabetes. So, three out of four have either diabetes or prediabetes. The rates are lower in smaller cities and villages, but even in villages, it has risen to 16% based on the INDAP studies.

In rural Kerala, diabetes prevalence is higher than in urban Kerala. What was once thought to be a “rich man’s disease” is no longer so. My father used to say, “We have the genes for diabetes, but when you go abroad, the environment brings it out.” Years ago, we wanted to test this hypothesis. At that time, prevalence among Indians in the U.S. or U.K. was much higher than in India. Now, with increased rates in India, we wanted to see if the hypothesis still held.

We collaborated with the University of California and Northern Chicago’s Masala Study in the U.S., using the same criteria as our Chennai data. Now, the prevalence in Chennai is as high as among migrant Indians in the U.S., so it’s no longer just a migrant phenomenon.

In 40 years, the situation has changed completely; India now has higher rates. When my daughter joined me, she asked, “Papa, why are you studying just Chennai and Delhi and then generalizing to India?” She wanted to study the whole country. With support from the ICMR, we conducted the largest epidemiological study on diabetes ever done, covering every state, union territory, and even islands like the Andaman & Nicobar. The study was published in Lancet in June 2023. It found 101 million people with diabetes in India and 136 million with prediabetes. Together, that’s 237 million people—a number larger than the population of most countries.

Providing comprehensive care to so many people is a huge challenge. More concerning than the numbers are the complications—if one-fifth develop heart problems or kidney disease, the need for treatment would be staggering. For example, 25% of people have early kidney disease—25 million people. If one-fifth progress to renal failure, that’s 5 million needing dialysis, but only 10,000–15,000 transplants are performed in India annually. That’s where Swami’s teaching—hari, wari, kari—comes in as the main causes of disease, which this conference has rightly emphasized.

The Asian Indian phenotype means we get type 2 diabetes much younger—20 years earlier than in the West. Heart disease also appears at a younger age. In 1985, when I worked at Hammersmith Hospital in London, there were no heart attacks below age 40 among white men, but 70% among South Asians and 30% among Afro-Caribbean people.

We have studied genetics extensively and published in Nature Medicine, showing South Asians do have genetic risk factors, but genetics alone cannot explain the epidemic. Environmental factors play a major role. In an interview, Swami asked me, “Did all your genes change since the 1970s? Why has diabetes increased?” He encouraged me to analyze my own data, and we found that carbohydrate consumption, especially rice, was a key driver.

We published our first study in the British Journal of Nutrition, showing a strong correlation between carbohydrate intake and diabetes in Chennai, even after adjusting for obesity, BMI, family history, socioeconomic status, and physical activity.

To further validate, we participated in the PURE study, following 130,000 people across five continents for 15 years. South Asia had the highest white rice intake, and a strong correlation was found between rice intake and new-onset diabetes. The hazard ratio was 1.61, meaning a 61% higher risk.

Our upcoming Nature Medicine paper will present state-by-state correlations between rice, wheat, oil consumption, and diabetes, including which dietary changes bring the most benefit. Good news: physical activity can reduce risk, even if rice intake is high. Among physically inactive people, risk is 50%, but with exercise, it drops to 16% and is no longer statistically significant.

Another paper by my daughter used mathematical modeling to determine how much carbohydrate should be reduced. This supports Swami’s advice that a vegetarian diet is best. Replacing carbohydrate with fat, however, showed no benefit—so we must reduce fat intake and increase protein, especially plant protein. Most people eat rice three times a day but don’t consume enough pulses, vegetables, and actual proteins.

Newer risk factors are also being studied. Dr. Prabhakaran and others have shown that pollution, particularly PM 2.5 particles, is an independent risk factor for hypertension, diabetes, cholesterol, and heart disease.

To conclude, India faces a diabetes epidemic closely tied to cardiovascular disease, but much of it is preventable. Simple steps like reducing carbohydrate, filling our plates with vegetables and fruit, and following Swami’s advice can help prevent both diabetes and heart disease. Once again, welcome to Prasanthi Nilayam. Thank you very much.

Hypertension Update 2025 in a Nutshell

Padma Shri Dr. C. Venkata S. Ram

Read transcript 1,540 words

Over the past 18 to 24 months, significant developments have occurred in the field of medicine, particularly regarding cardiovascular disease. I will provide a concise overview of our current knowledge, its relevance to future medical practice, and the urgent need to contain the growing pandemic of cardiovascular disease, especially in India.

Ladies and gentlemen, the financial burden of cardiovascular disease in India over the preceding decade has been enormous. When converted to rupees, the expenditure amounts to 20 lakh crores. These costs were incurred to treat individuals with cardiovascular disease—conditions that could have been prevented through aggressive management of cardiovascular risk factors. Unlike most countries where the cardiovascular disease burden is decreasing, South Asia, and India in particular, is experiencing an increase. We are moving in the opposite direction compared to the rest of the world. While there are multiple reasons for this trend—which I will not elaborate on here—we must recognize that the “time bomb” of cardiovascular disease continues to progress, despite the availability of many therapeutic approaches.

Hypertension is the most significant cause of atherosclerosis. While hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and tobacco use are also important, high blood pressure remains the leading contributor. Many of you may be familiar with coarctation of the aorta. In this condition, atherosclerosis develops above the coarctation, but not below it. This simple biological phenomenon illustrates that blood pressure is elevated above the coarctation and normal below it, resulting in atherosclerosis only in the area of high pressure. This clearly confirms that high blood pressure is a key driver of atherosclerosis.

Although Dr. Krishna Rao requested that I focus on developments from the last two years, I will briefly revisit the past 100 years regarding optimal blood pressure targets. Nearly a century ago, it was proposed that a blood pressure of 120/80 mmHg was associated with longer life expectancy, while higher levels were linked to reduced longevity. Remarkably, current guidelines in 2025 once again recommend a target blood pressure close to 120/80 mmHg. What insurance companies advocated 100 years ago remains true today.

Until three years ago, hypertension was defined as a blood pressure of around 130/80 mmHg. Now, new recommendations suggest aiming for 120/80 mmHg if possible. Patients and their relatives often ask whether they can use salt substitutes when advised to reduce salt intake. While we once casually permitted salt substitutes to retain flavor, overwhelming evidence now shows that they can lower blood pressure, reduce cardiovascular disease, decrease mortality, and reduce stroke risk. Pure salt is 99% sodium chloride, while salt substitutes contain 60–70% potassium chloride, with the remainder being sodium chloride. These substitutes preserve taste and, according to new studies, not only lower blood pressure but also reduce mortality.

A recent study involving 21,000 patients compared normal salt to salt substitutes. Those using salt substitutes experienced significantly lower blood pressure, reduced risk of cardiovascular events, and decreased mortality. Thus, when advising patients to use salt substitutes, we now have scientific evidence supporting their benefits for blood pressure and target organ protection.

Let me now discuss the rapid development of new molecules for hypertension treatment over the past decade—progress that exceeds the advancements made in the previous 70 to 80 years. These new drugs target the underlying pathophysiology of hypertension.

One such molecule, the endothelin antagonist, has been approved in the United States. Endothelin is the most potent vasoconstrictor in humans, 10,000 times stronger than noradrenaline. Blocking both endothelin A and B receptors leads to a substantial reduction in blood pressure. A recent U.S. study showed remarkable blood pressure reductions in patients treated with endothelin antagonists, marking a significant advance in hypertension therapy.

Other new molecules target the renin-angiotensin system. While ACE inhibitors and ARBs are well-known, newer drugs aim to block the actual formation of angiotensin. By inhibiting angiotensin generation, the entire cascade is suppressed. One such drug is an MRA inhibitor called zelbisiran, which blocks angiotensin production in the liver. A single injection of zelbisiran can lower blood pressure for six to nine months or longer. Studies show zelbisiran reduces angiotensin and significantly lowers blood pressure. Importantly, if excessive hypotension occurs, its effects can be reversed with a drug called reversar. Thus, long-lasting antihypertensive effects can be controlled as needed.

In addition to blocking angiotensin, efforts are underway to inhibit aldosterone synthesis—not just the receptor. Aldosterone synthase inhibitors are being developed to address the root mechanisms of hypertension. These drugs have demonstrated efficacy in lowering blood pressure.

Overall, we are moving from “irrational” therapy to rational approaches that target the specific mechanisms responsible for hypertension. The development of these molecules is progressing rapidly, and many will become available during our lifetimes.

For those in long-term clinical practice, a common debate has centered around whether hydrochlorothiazide or chlorthalidone is the better diuretic. Past studies showed chlorthalidone lowered blood pressure slightly more than hydrochlorothiazide but lacked outcome data. Last year, an outcome study revealed that both drugs equally lower blood pressure and affect potassium. However, patients on chlorthalidone experienced better outcomes, including a lower incidence of heart failure. Therefore, while both drugs are effective for hypertension, chlorthalidone is slightly superior for preventing cardiovascular disease and heart failure, settling this longstanding debate.

Recently, new U.S. guidelines for hypertension were released on August 14. It remains uncertain whether these guidelines are applicable in India, as Indian guidelines are expected by December. The U.S. guidelines now define two stages of hypertension and encourage lowering blood pressure to less than 120/80 mmHg—previously 130/80 mmHg—a major shift from earlier recommendations. Achieving this goal depends on accurate blood pressure measurement.

Additionally, the term “hypertensive urgency” has been removed from the guidelines; it is now referred to as “severe hypertension.” The guidelines also confirm a strong link between blood pressure and cognitive dysfunction. Uncontrolled hypertension is the number one cause of cognitive decline, whether classified as Alzheimer’s disease or vascular dementia.

How did experts determine the benefit of a blood pressure target of 120 mmHg? One pivotal trial showed that patients who achieved blood pressure levels below 120 mmHg had the greatest benefit.

In closing, I want to comment briefly on the growing role of artificial intelligence in diagnosing and treating hypertension. Research in this area is expanding rapidly, with numerous publications and new models under development. The application of artificial intelligence may lead to more rational and appropriate hypertension management. However, as Bill Gates has noted, it must be managed carefully to ensure the benefits outweigh the risks. While AI will not replace physicians, those who utilize AI may surpass those who do not. There are still unresolved issues regarding whether AI models can replicate human judgment, data access, and data custodianship—whether the computer, AI vendor, insurance company, or patient should be responsible for the data. Legal questions remain, but the application of AI in hypertension is being widely studied.

Mr. Chairman, ladies and gentlemen, in the time allocated, I have provided a rapid overview of developments from the past two years. I discussed the evidence for salt substitutes, the use of endothelin antagonists, long-acting drugs that block the renin-angiotensin system, aldosterone synthase inhibitors, and the resolved debate between hydrochlorothiazide and chlorthalidone. Finally, I touched on artificial intelligence in hypertension. Thank you for your attention, and thanks to Swami for giving me this privilege. Thank you very much.

Answer:

According to current guidelines, both types of diuretics are equally effective. However, comparative studies have shown the superiority of certain options. If the patient's blood pressure is stable and they are doing well, I don't think there is any need to change the existing therapy at all.

Answer:
